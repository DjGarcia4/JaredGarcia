[SwiftFlow](/project/swiftflow-typing-test) is a typing test in English and Spanish that doesn't just measure you: it tells you which keys, fingers and words slow you down, and builds the practice to fix them. It's free, has no ads, asks for no account, and everything stays in your browser. You open it at [swiftflowtyping.netlify.app](https://swiftflowtyping.netlify.app) and start typing.

This guide goes in the order it's worth using it: first your keyboard, then the course if you never learned to type without looking, and then daily practice guided by what your data says. Button and menu names are quoted as they appear in the app's English interface.

## 1. Pick your keyboard layout

SwiftFlow draws the on-screen keyboard, colors the fingers and builds its tips from your layout. If it doesn't match yours, it will point you to the wrong fingers and keys.

| Layout | Who it's for |
| --- | --- |
| Latin American | Spanish keyboards sold in Latin America |
| Spanish (Spain) | Spanish keyboards from Spain |
| US International | English keyboards, with dead keys for accents |
| Dvorak | If you type on Dvorak |
| Colemak | If you type on Colemak |

<p class="tbl-note">Until you pick one, the app guesses from the browser's language: Spain for <code>es-ES</code> or Catalan, US for English and Latin American for everyone else.</p>

You change it from “Keyboard layout” on the on-screen keyboard (show it with the keyboard button under the text), from the history, or on the course page under “For your keyboard:”. You can also open the commands with Ctrl/⌘ K and type `dvorak`.

## 2. Starting from zero: the course

If you still look at the keyboard, start with “Course from scratch” (the graduation-cap icon at the top, or the O key). It's 24 lessons: one row at a time, one pair of fingers at a time.

| Stage | Lessons | Speed goal |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Home row | 5 finger pairs + “Review” | 8 wpm (review: 10) |
| Top row | 5 finger pairs + “Review” | 12 wpm (review: 14) |
| Bottom row | 5 finger pairs + “Review” | 14 wpm (review: 16) |
| Beyond the letters | Capitals, Accents, Numbers 1 to 5, Numbers 6 to 0, Signs, Final test | 10 to 20 wpm |

<p class="tbl-note">Each row starts with the index fingers and moves outward to the pinkies; the fifth pair is the index fingers stretching toward the middle.</p>

The rules are simple and worth knowing:

- A lesson is passed at its speed goal and at least 94% accuracy. Passing it opens the next one.
- One star for passing; two if you also reach 98% accuracy; three with 98% and one and a half times the speed goal.
- Each lesson only uses keys you've already learned: letter groups at first and, as soon as there are enough, real words.
- Finger colors are always on during a lesson.

If you fail on accuracy, the app tells you: “You need 94% accuracy: slower and clean pays off more.” Listen to it. At this stage, slow and clean is worth more than fast and fixing. When you finish, S takes you to the next lesson and R or Space repeat it.

## 3. Everyday practice: Time and Words

  <figcaption>The mode menu (shown in Spanish): each mode with a line on what it is and its key.</figcaption>
</figure>

For day-to-day practice, the two base modes are:

- Time: 15, 30, 60 or 120 seconds of continuous text. Good for measuring yourself the same way every time.
- Words: 10, 25, 50 or 100 words, built into sentences. It ends when you type the last one.

In “Options” you turn punctuation on or off (“Capitals, accents and marks, like a real text”). If you're just starting, turn it off; once you type comfortably, turn it on, because that's what you'll type in real life.

While you type you see net WPM, which only counts complete, correct words, like Monkeytype. At the end, raw WPM shows up too, counting everything you typed: the gap between the two is what your mistakes cost you. Accuracy counts every keystroke, even mistakes you fixed with backspace.

If you stop typing for 3 seconds, the run pauses on its own and resumes when you type again. Esc pauses on purpose and a second Esc ends it; a run cut short that way isn't saved to your history.

Besides the four big numbers, look at these:

- “consistency”: how even your pace was, second by second. 100% is a metronome.
- “Where did you slow down?” (F): each word against your pace in that run, with your slowest words and a “Train these” button.
- The “WPM by second” chart, with mistakes marked.

For the next run, press Space. If you want to share the result, C opens the card, with a different design when it's a record.

## 5. “What to improve”: what your data says

After a few runs, open the history (H) and go to the “Your keys” chapter. That's where “What to improve” is: up to four tips built from your own data. If there isn't enough yet, it says so: “Play a couple more runs and I'll tell you what to focus on.”

What it can tell you:

- Which keys you miss most for how often you use them, and which key you mix them up with.
- Whether your fingers get ahead of you (you swap the order of two letters).
- Whether one hand, row or finger misses more than the rest.
- Which keys and pairs slow you down even though you don't miss them.
- Which whole words trip you up.

The same chapter has “Most missed keys” (a keyboard map over the last 30 sessions) and “How your keys are going”, which compares your latest runs with the ones before. The “Your fingers” chapter shows each finger's error rate and speed and marks the one that lags.

## 6. Train what's weak: Train and Fingers

The tips have buttons that take you to practice. You can also go straight there:

- Train: builds the text around your weakest keys, from your history. You can pick others, up to 5. The drill tells you when to stop: “Done for today with these letters!” when they've come down enough, or “That's enough for today” if you've gone several rounds without improving.
- Fingers: you pick which fingers to practice in a hand-shaped picker, with shortcuts for “Left hand”, “Right hand”, “Both hands”, “Index fingers” and “Pinkies”. The text comes only from those fingers' letters on your keyboard, with real words when there are any.

The letters you drill go into “Letters in review”: they come back at 1, 3, 7, 14 and 30 days as long as they hold up. If one clearly gets worse, it starts over. When a review is due, you'll see it in the history's “Today” chapter.

During a normal run, if a letter keeps slipping, a notice tells you and offers “Train now” (D on the results).

## 7. When you want to push: the demanding modes

“Options” has modes that combine with any mode:

| Option | What it does | What it's for |
| --- | --- | --- |
| No net | Mistakes aren't shown until the end | Stop fixing while staring at the screen |
| Sudden death | The first mistake ends the run | Force yourself to be accurate |
| Fix to move on | A wrong letter doesn't go in: you have to get it right to go on | Train correcting in the moment |
| Minimum accuracy (90, 95 or 98%) | Below it, the run doesn't count | Set yourself an accuracy floor |

<p class="tbl-note">Sudden death and Fix to move on are alternatives to each other. A run that fails isn't saved; one that makes it is flagged in the history.</p>

Use them when your accuracy is already high and you want to hold it. If you're barely over 90%, slowing down beats turning on sudden death.

## 8. Adjust how it looks: focus, typography and accessibility

The “Aa” button under the text opens “How the text looks”, with a live sample:

- Font: Monospace, JetBrains Mono, Nunito, Atkinson Hyperlegible or OpenDyslexic.
- Size (four options) and line spacing (“Tight”, “Normal”, “Loose”).
- Caret: “Glides” or “Jumps”.
- “Focus mode”: just the word you're typing and the next one, large and centered.
- “High contrast”, across the app.

If seeing the upcoming text distracts you, try focus mode. If reading is hard for you, Atkinson Hyperlegible and OpenDyslexic are there for that. The whole app works from the keyboard, dialogs give the focus back when they close, and results, achievements and tips are announced to screen readers.

## 9. Everything without leaving the keyboard

Reaching for the mouse breaks your rhythm. That's why every button shows its key next to it: on the results it's the key alone, and during the test it goes with Alt (⌥ on Mac).

| Key | What it does |
| --- | --- |
| Ctrl/⌘ K | Opens the commands: type `30`, `words 50`, `dark`, `dvorak`, `history`… |
| Tab | Starts over with another text, mid-run |
| Esc | Pauses; again, ends the run |
| Space | Another run, when you're done |
| ? | The shortcut list (outside the test) |
| M, 1–9, W | In the test, with Alt/⌥: the mode menu, duration or count, and “Options” |
| R, F, G, B, C, L | On the results: another run, where you slowed down, ghost, pacer, share, challenges |
| P, O, H, Z, T | The top bar: the test, the course, the history, sound and language, theme |

<p class="tbl-note">Inside every open menu, each option has its letter written next to it and needs no Alt.</p>

## 10. Challenges, achievements and goals

To make practice a habit:

- “Today's challenges”: three daily challenges (L), tuned to your level from your previous sessions.
- The weekly challenge: 40 words, the same text for everyone that week.
- “Weekly goal”: minutes of practice from Monday to Sunday (30, 60, 90, 120 or 180).
- Your streak: if you haven't practiced today, the app warns you before midnight. You can ask for an evening reminder, which arrives if SwiftFlow is open, even in another tab.
- 94 achievements and 50 levels; leveling up unlocks colors, caret styles and sounds.

Two more tools for competing with yourself: the ghost (G), which runs at your record's pace, and the pacer (B), which goes at a fixed speed.

## 11. Track your progress: history and Your summary

The history is split into nine chapters: “Your level”, “Today”, “Your speed”, “Your records”, “Your keys”, “Your fingers”, “Your habits”, “Achievements” and “Your runs”. Keys 1 to 9 take you to each one. In “Your speed”, filter by a mode to compare runs of the same kind; the trend tells you where you're heading by comparing your last 10 runs with the 10 before. In “Your records”, “Race it” opens that category with the pacer at your record's speed.

“Your summary” tells your month or your year: runs, minutes, record, average against the period before, the key you tamed and the new achievements. “Share my summary” turns it into an image.

Since everything lives in your browser, use “Export” in “Your runs” now and then. If you clear the browser's data, that file is the only copy; it also lets you move your history to another device with “Import”.

## 12. Install it as an app

On the “What is SwiftFlow” page (Q) there's an “Install app” button in browsers that allow it; in Safari or Firefox you install it from the browser's menu. Once installed, it opens in its own window and works offline.

## A weekly routine (suggestion)

This is only a suggestion for organizing your practice, not a promise of results. Adjust it to your time and to what “What to improve” tells you.

| Day | What to do | Approximate time |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Monday | One course lesson (or, if you've finished it, Words 50) and the review in “Today” | 10 min |
| Tuesday | Time 60 a couple of times, then Train on your weak keys | 10 min |
| Wednesday | Fingers with the finger “Your fingers” flags | 10 min |
| Thursday | Words 25 with punctuation and Minimum accuracy 95% | 10 min |
| Friday | The weekly challenge and today's challenges | 10 min |
| Saturday | Quote or Classics, in focus mode | 10 min |
| Sunday | Look at “Your keys” and “Your speed” and pick what to train next week | 5 min |

<p class="tbl-note">About 65 minutes a week: enough for the 60-minute “Weekly goal”. It's a suggestion, not a rule of the app.</p>

> If you keep only one idea, make it this: don't chase WPM directly. Type clean, let “What to improve” tell you what slows you down, and train exactly that. That is what SwiftFlow is built for.
